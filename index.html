CHAPTER 01PART I · THE NEURON

From a circuit
to a spike.

The leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron is a remarkably compact electrical abstraction: a capacitor stores charge, a resistor leaks it, and a threshold mechanism produces a discrete event.

Equivalent RC circuit of a LIF neuronSCHEMATIC · NOT TO SCALE

Conceptual current-based LIF circuit. A comparator observes membrane voltage; when the membrane reaches threshold, a spike is emitted and the reset switch returns voltage to its reset value. This is a behavioral schematic, not a transistor-level design.

SPIKING RULE · THRESHOLD THEN RESET

Compare the membrane voltage with the threshold, not the other way around: when Vm ≥ Vth, the neuron emits a spike and resets to Vreset. For Vm < Vth, no spike is emitted; current continues to charge the capacitor while the resistor leaks charge away. The comparator output also drives the reset switch (dashed feedback connection). The live graph below plots this sequence as repeated ramp-to-threshold and instantaneous reset events.

\[s(t)=H(V_m(t)-V_{th}),\qquad s(t)=1\;\Rightarrow\; V_m(t^+)=V_{reset}\]

Kirchhoff's current law

The input current splits between the membrane capacitor and the leak resistance. Using Ohm's law and \(I_C=C_m\,dV_m/dt\):

\[C_m\frac{dV_m}{dt}=I_{in}(t)-\frac{V_m-E_L}{R_m}\]

The membrane time constant

Define \(\tau_m=R_m C_m\). Resistance determines how easily charge escapes; capacitance determines how much charge is needed to change voltage. With fixed input the membrane approaches \(E_L+R_m I_{in}\); if that level remains below threshold, the model does not spike. When it is above threshold, successive threshold crossings create spikes followed by resets.

\[\tau_m\frac{dV_m}{dt}=-(V_m-E_L)+R_m I_{in}(t)\]

CHAPTER 02PART I · BACKGROUND

Before the voltage:
synaptic current.

Conductance- or current-filtered synaptic models introduce an additional state: a spike generates synaptic current, which decays over time and then drives the membrane. This separates the timescale of the synapse from the timescale of the membrane.

Synaptic current filter

In continuous time, \(\tau_{syn}dI/dt=-I\) between spike arrivals, giving exponential decay. In a discrete spike-driven model, incoming weighted spikes add to the retained current.

\[I_{syn}[t+1]=\beta_{syn}I_{syn}[t]+\sum_i w_i s_i[t]\] \[\beta_{syn}=e^{-\Delta t/\tau_{syn}}\]

Membrane filter

The synaptic current is then integrated by the membrane. A common reduced model measures voltage relative to rest and absorbs the gain into current units.

\[\tau_m\frac{du}{dt}=-u+I_{syn}(t)\]

CHAPTER 03PART I · THE NEURON

Neurons with
memory.

LIF provides integration and decay; its extensions let neurons learn their response timescales or remember previous activity through a second state variable.

WHY LEARN NEURON DYNAMICS?

From a fixed integrator to adaptive temporal computation

A fixed LIF membrane has one characteristic integration timescale. Fang et al. (2021) make that timescale a trained parameter — one value per layer, shared by that layer’s neurons — so different layers can specialize in fast or slow temporal signals instead of sharing a hand-picked constant. They report that such networks are less sensitive to the initial time constant and learn faster. Deckers et al. (2024) add the adaptation state because a neuron’s response to a new input can depend on its recent voltage and spike history. In their experiments, letting the voltage-feedback coefficient a go negative could push a neuron into a chaotic regime where positive feedback keeps it spiking even without input, so they bound a and b to positive ranges (their AdLIF+ variant; this guide abbreviates constrained AdLIF as cAdLIF). A positive a acts as a homeostatic brake: the higher the voltage was in the past, the more spiking is suppressed. On the SHD speech benchmark, average validation accuracy rose from 84.5% (plain LIF) to 91.7% (AdLIF) and 94.2% (AdLIF+). The constraint is a design choice validated on speech tasks, not a universal stability theorem.

CHAPTER 04PART II · LEARNING

Teaching a spike
to differentiate.

The spike function \(s=H(u-\theta)\) is discontinuous. Its ordinary derivative is zero away from threshold and undefined at threshold, making naïve gradient descent unhelpful. Surrogate-gradient training leaves hard spikes in the forward pass while replacing the derivative in the backward pass.

WHY THIS WORKS — AND WHEN IT CAN FAIL

Hard spikes forward, smooth teaching signal backward

Direct differentiation through a binary threshold gives essentially no usable gradient for learning synaptic weights. Surrogate gradients replace only the backward derivative so gradient-based training can still credit or blame weights for spikes. The local gradient is strongest near threshold: widening the surrogate gives more neurons a learning signal but changes the approximation. Li et al. (2021) study this choice by analysing training with a finite-difference gradient, and propose Dspike, a family of surrogate functions whose shape and smoothness evolve during training. In recurrent SNNs, gradients also pass through many time steps; truncation, decay, and reset influence whether useful signals survive.

\[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial s}\,\underbrace{\frac{\partial s}{\partial u}}_{\text{surrogate in backprop}}\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial w}\]

PAPER FINDING

Li et al. argue that the heuristic choice of surrogate leaves open how it actually helps training, so they analyse gradient descent with a finite-difference gradient and propose Dspike, whose shape adapts during training. They report 75.4% top-1 accuracy on CIFAR10-DVS with a spiking ResNet-18 at 10 time steps. General intuition (not a claim of the paper): a very narrow surrogate gives few neurons any learning signal, while a broad one drifts further from the true threshold.

A second difficulty is that even with a surrogate derivative, signal and gradient variance can vanish or explode as network depth grows. Chapter 5 shows one way to handle this: rescaling the surrogate, \(H'(u)\mapsto kH'(u)\), to regulate backward variance. The right \(k\) depends on the model and assumptions rather than being a universal constant.

CHAPTER 05PART II · LEARNING

The hidden art
of starting well.

A good SNN initialization must satisfy two intertwined goals: sufficient spiking activity in the forward pass and stable gradient propagation in the backward pass.

FORWARD PASS

Avoid firing-rate collapse

Weak random drive can leave most neurons silent; excessive drive can create pathological high activity. Target activity statistics can guide weight selection.

BACKWARD PASS

Keep gradients usable

Layer depth, decay, and surrogate derivative scales determine gradient variance. Initialization may need explicit variance propagation analysis.

The two approaches below answer the same question at different depths. Rossbroich et al. (2022) give a practical, data-dependent recipe aimed at fluctuation-driven firing. Perez-Nieves and Goodman (2023) cite that work and extend the analysis to the threshold-and-reset mechanism, the effect of discrete timesteps, and an analytical result for the backward pass.

APPROACH A · PEREZ-NIEVES & GOODMAN (2023)

Forward firing-rate collapse: why ANN initializers are not enough

In conventional ANNs, keeping activation variance constant from layer to layer (as in Kaiming initialization) is a good starting point. Perez-Nieves and Goodman show that it does not carry over: applying the same variance argument to an SNN’s forward pass only bounds the firing probability from above, which can still mean no firing at all. Predicting firing rates instead requires the distribution of membrane voltage near threshold, which they take from the diffusion (Fokker–Planck) and shot-noise approximations of a LIF neuron.

Those approximations assume continuous time, but SNNs trained with deep-learning tools are simulated in discrete steps of about 1 ms, roughly ten times coarser than in computational neuroscience. The authors call the resulting fall in simulated firing rate as the step grows firing-rate collapse; it is already substantial near 1 ms. Their explanation is about input ordering: a discrete step adds excitatory and inhibitory inputs together before checking the threshold, so a neuron that would have crossed threshold part-way through the step and then been pulled back down is counted as silent. They correct for this with random-walk, Wiener-process and permutation-sampling methods, and note that the diffusion approximation also needs small weights (shot-noise generalizes it to larger, exponentially distributed ones).

Backward propagation: why the surrogate derivative needs its own scale

During backpropagation through time, gradients multiply synaptic weights, membrane-state Jacobians, and the chosen surrogate derivatives. Repeating these multiplications can shrink gradient variance toward zero or amplify it across layers and time. The paper applies variance-propagation reasoning to the backward pass (its Theorem 6); in the notation used here (the paper writes κ for the surrogate scale), the balance and the scale it implies are:

\[\operatorname{Var}(g_{\mathrm{in}})\;\approx\;\frac{n_{\mathrm{out}}\sigma_w^2\,k^2\,\mathcal I}{1-\alpha^2}\;\operatorname{Var}(g_{\mathrm{out}}),\qquad \mathcal I=\int [H'(v)]^2\hat p(v)\,dv\]
\[\operatorname{Var}(g_{\mathrm{in}})\approx\operatorname{Var}(g_{\mathrm{out}})\quad\Rightarrow\quad k\approx\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha^2}{n_{\mathrm{out}}\sigma_w^2\mathcal I}}\]

This is a variance-balance approximation derived under stated assumptions, not an exact general formula: it assumes zero-mean independent weights, gradients that are uncorrelated across time, and a narrow surrogate (the paper’s Assumption 13). Here \(n_{\mathrm{out}}\) is fan-out, \(\sigma_w^2\) the weight variance, \(\alpha\) a membrane retention factor, \(H'\) the unscaled surrogate derivative, and \(\hat p(v)\) the membrane-voltage distribution just before the reset is applied (not the steady-state density, because the surrogate acts on the voltage before reset). The key insight is that a forward-stable spike rate and a backward-stable gradient are distinct constraints. Changing \(k\) scales the learning signal without changing the forward spike rule, whereas changing weight variance generally alters both the voltage distribution and the backward signal.

Research limitation: the derivations assume LIF neurons with delta synapses and homogeneous Poisson input, and were tested on simulated 20-layer feed-forward networks, not real datasets. Perez-Nieves and Goodman state that transferring their prescriptions to real datasets remains an open question, so the equations should guide diagnostic measurements, not replace them.

APPROACH B · ROSSBROICH, GYGAX & ZENKE (2022)

Fluctuation-driven initialization: why keep the mean below threshold?

Rossbroich, Gygax and Zenke (2022) argue for a biologically motivated operating regime in which the average membrane voltage can remain subthreshold while noise-like fluctuations occasionally cross the firing threshold. This avoids the extremes of silence and persistent strongly driven firing and leaves neurons sensitive to time-varying input. Their initialization is data-dependent and predicts membrane-voltage fluctuations using input statistics and postsynaptic-potential (PSP) filters.

\[\xi=\frac{\theta-\mu_u}{\sigma_u},\qquad \bar\epsilon=\int_0^\infty\epsilon(t)\,dt,\qquad \hat\epsilon=\int_0^\infty\epsilon(t)^2\,dt\]

A smaller positive \(\xi\) means the threshold lies nearer the mean in units of standard deviation, making threshold crossings more likely under a Gaussian snapshot approximation. With independent incoming spikes, estimated input rate, fan-in, and PSP filter integrals can be used to pick weight statistics producing a desired fluctuation level. The strategy is not just a firing-rate heuristic: the authors report improved learning with surrogate-gradient backpropagation across several architectures. That benefit does not automatically establish constant gradient variance at every depth; forward responsiveness and backward variance remain separate diagnostics. The authors keep ξ in roughly the range 1–3 and, for deep networks, add firing-rate homeostasis, which adjusts the initial weights to restore activity propagation; they test fully connected, convolutional, recurrent and Dale’s-law networks.

Practical reading (a synthesis, not a prescription from either paper): First tune weights to put neurons in a responsive fluctuation regime, then inspect gradient magnitudes during training and adjust the surrogate scale or optimization setup if needed. Too-small variance causes silence; too-large variance increases uncontrolled threshold crossings and makes learning less reliable.

CHAPTER 06PART III · TIME

Learning when
to listen.

In an SNN, a connection can carry not just a weight \(w_{ij}\), but also a delay \(d_{ij}\). Time shifts can align or separate spike patterns and therefore alter the resulting postsynaptic response.

\[I_i^{\ell}[t]=\sum_j F_{ij}^{\ell-1}\,s_j^{\ell-1}[t-d_{ij}]+b_i^{\ell}\]
SYNAPTIC

Per connection

A → B can be delayed 2 ms, A → C by 5 ms, and A → D by 8 ms.

AXONAL

Shared outbound delay

One neuron's output may be delayed by 5 ms before branching to its targets.

WHY LEARN TIMING?

Temporal coincidence can be more informative than spike count

In speech or other time-varying data, two events may carry information through their order and separation even if their total counts match. Learnable synaptic delays shift incoming events in time, helping downstream neurons detect coincidences or sequence patterns. Deckers et al. (2024) learn weights, adaptation parameters and a separate delay for every synapse together; Vassallo and Taherinejad (2026) instead derive local three-factor rules so delays can be learned online. Individual synaptic delays offer more flexibility than a single shared axonal delay but also increase the parameter and memory budget.

Offline: delays through BPTT

Deckers et al. (2024) adapt SLAYER’s delay learning to individual synapses: the delayed spike signal is differentiable in time, so the gradient with respect to a delay comes from its time derivative, trained with backpropagation through time. Delays start at zero and are capped at 25 time steps. A two-layer, 128-unit network reached 96.26% on SHD with about 76k parameters. In their ablation, delays lifted the average SHD validation accuracy of a plain LIF network from 84.5% to 92.2%, but of an AdLIF+ network only from 94.2% to 95.0%: the two features are complementary, though not additive.

Paper ↗

Online: three-factor rules

Vassallo and Taherinejad (2026) build on e-prop: each parameter keeps a local eligibility trace of recent pre- and postsynaptic activity, and it is updated only when a top-down error signal (the third factor) modulates that trace, so nothing is unrolled through time. They learn both synaptic and axonal delays and use a smooth Gaussian surrogate only inside the eligibility trace. They report up to 18% higher accuracy than weights-only learning, and up to 14% higher for networks with similar parameter counts. On SHD, accuracy is similar to offline backpropagation-based approaches; relative to state-of-the-art methods the model is 6.6× smaller with 50% lower inference latency and a 2.5% drop in accuracy.

Paper ↗
IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

Having delays is not the same as learning delays. A properly chosen fixed delay can already improve temporal alignment; learnable delays bring flexibility but add optimization and implementation cost. In Deckers et al., giving every synapse its own delay doubled the connection parameters (about 38k to 75k for the LIF baseline).

Benchmark context: SHD (Spiking Heidelberg Digits) and SSC (Spiking Speech Commands) are event-based spoken-language classification datasets, commonly used benchmarks for temporally structured spike processing.

CHAPTER 07PART IV · EFFICIENCY

Doing more
with less.

Several complementary approaches improve efficiency: reduce unnecessary timesteps, remove low-utility neurons, and search for compact network configurations.

WHY THE EFFICIENCY METHODS DIFFER

Reduce time, activity, or model size

Mixed-timestep methods allocate fewer simulation steps to layers that can tolerate lower temporal resolution; temporal alignment is then needed to pass information between layers with unlike step counts. Time inheritance seeks cheaper training by reusing weights across timestep schedules rather than starting every configuration from scratch. Activity-based pruning removes infrequently firing neurons to lower work and storage, but spike count alone need not equal causal importance. Evolutionary search instead explores architectural alternatives without requiring a differentiable parameterization. All four methods involve trade-offs with accuracy and hardware-dependent latency.

01 · MIXED-TIMESTEP NETWORKS

Different layers, different simulation lengths

Guo et al. (2026) build the SNN by converting a trained ANN. They show that a layer’s SNN timestep count can be approximated by the number of activation levels of the source ANN (\(T_\ell\approx 2^{n_\ell}\) for an \(n_\ell\)-bit activation), so layer-wise bit-widths map to layer-wise timesteps, and they optimize classification loss alongside a temporal cost. Adjacent layers with different lengths must be aligned; their Scaled Synaptic Current Accumulation (SSCA) folds that alignment into the synaptic-current computation instead of adding a separate step.

\[J=\mathcal L\!\left(Q(a;\eta)\right)+\lambda\sum_{\ell}t^{\ell}\]

The published ResNet-34 result reported by the authors is 73.63% ImageNet-1K top-1 accuracy at 4.88 average timesteps. This is the authors' reported benchmark, not a reproduction. For comparison, the source ANN scores 74.01%, so the gap is 0.38 points. The paper’s energy comparisons are theoretical arithmetic estimates, not measured hardware energy.

Time inheritance training (TIT)

Deng et al. (2022) first propose TET, a loss that supervises the network’s output at every timestep rather than only the time-averaged output, aiming for flatter minima and better generalization. Because TET makes networks work across simulation lengths, they add TIT: train for many epochs at a short length (for example T = 2), then raise T and fine-tune for fewer epochs. In their worked example this roughly halves the training cost of a T = 6 network.

Paper ↗

Adaptive neuron pruning

Guo et al. (2020) work with unsupervised, STDP-trained spiking networks. They treat neurons that fire more as more important and prune low-activity ones, including an online version whose spike-count threshold adapts during training. Pruning 400 of 800 neurons gave 2.3× less memory and 2.8× better energy efficiency for a 1.69% loss in accuracy. Spike count is a heuristic for importance, not a measured causal effect.

ALTERNATIVE OPTIMIZATION

Evolutionary Optimization for Neuromorphic Systems (EONS)

EONS evaluates a population of candidate SNNs by fitness, then selects and mutates stronger candidates to produce new generations. This provides a non-gradient-based route to architectural search and hardware-aware design. Schuman et al. (2020) present an updated, Python-based version of a framework first introduced in 2016; it can determine a network’s structure and/or parameters, train under hardware constraints, evolve reservoirs for liquid state machines, and, with multi-objective optimization, evolve smaller networks.
